Illustrative example
1What the child already doesWith one familiar object and its matching picture in view, the child points to the match after the educator names it. This is an observed strength with adult support.

2Where the activity stopsWith six pictures on a worksheet and three spoken instructions together—find, circle, then put away—the child looks between the sheet and the adult and stops. The observation does not tell us why.

3The barrier to investigateThe educator first checks whether the number of choices and instruction sequence make access harder. This is a working explanation to test, not a label for the child.

4The adaptation we chooseKeep the same familiar matching goal. Present two large picture cards, say “Point to the ball” and accept pointing as the response. Offer time, help and a way to pause.
If the child chooses with this supportKeep the accessible format, record the help still needed and review the next small step with the educator. Do not assume the ability has transferred to a worksheet or classroom.

If the child pauses or turns awayRespect the pause. Revisit communication, comfort, materials and the goal with the professional; another discipline may contribute where indicated. More pressure is not the next step.
The response to the two-choice, one-instruction adaptation is not yet observed. Supported matching is not an independent response or evidence of classroom transfer.
