Illustrative example
1Start with what the child showsImagine a child who points to choose blocks, but covers their ears and moves away when a noisy group activity begins. The family wants their child to be heard and feel able to join play.

2Make the request accessibleAfter checking contributing needs, the professional and family agree a familiar gesture or picture for “break”. Adults recognise that request and offer a quieter space; a break is not earned by complying first.

3Describe what happened, including helpIn this illustration, the child uses the break picture after an adult points to it, takes a pause and later chooses blocks beside one peer. The record includes the adult cue and quieter setting.

4Let that observation change the next stepThe team keeps the cue available and plans another supported opportunity in a familiar routine. It has not yet established an independent request or comfortable participation in a noisy group.
A request works with supportRecord the cue, setting and response. Keep the useful support and review whether the request becomes accessible across another relevant routine. Independence is a separate observation.

Discomfort or difficulty continuesPause the demand and revisit communication, health, sensory and environmental factors with the relevant professional. Change the plan or seek another assessment rather than simply repeat the task.
Independent requesting or comfortable participation in a noisy group; the next supported opportunity is still to be observed.
